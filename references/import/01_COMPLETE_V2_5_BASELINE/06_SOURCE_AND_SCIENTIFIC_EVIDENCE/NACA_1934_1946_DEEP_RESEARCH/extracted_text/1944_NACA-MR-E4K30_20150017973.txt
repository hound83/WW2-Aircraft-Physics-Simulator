                                      SUNMARY

            Bench tests have been made of an Allison V-1710-93 engine at
      simulated altitudes of 17,000 and29,000 feet to determine the per-
      formance of the engine, the characteristics of.the induction sys-
      tem, and the modifications required in the induction system for the
    • development of 1840 brake horsepower at 17,030 feet and 1200 horse-
      power at 29000 feet. With the engine speed maintained at a constant
      value of 3000 rpm, the ti p speed of the auxiliary-stage supercharger
      was varied from the minimum to the maximum speed. Measurements were
      made to determine engine power, fuel and air consumptions, and pres-
      sure and temperature changes introduced by each component of the induc-
      tion system.

           Although the over-all pressure ratios of the surerhargers agreed
     with those determined from previous sea-level bench tests of these
     units, the over-all temperature rise was -generally much lower than had
     been anticipated. In order to obtain the desired brake horsepower,
     some form of charge-air cooling is essential; the carburetor should
     either be enlarged or removed; and'th& minimum gear ratios of the
     engine-stage and the auxiliary-stage superchargers should be 8.1:1
     and 8.08:1, respectively.


                                   INTRODUCTION

          At the requst of the Army Air Forces, Air Technical Service Com-
     mand, the Cleveland laboratory of the- NACA is conducting an investiga-
     tion to improve the altitude performance of the Allison V- 1 790-93 engine.
     Brake horsepowers approaching 1540 at 17,000 feet and 1200 at 29,000 feet
     were the immediate objective.
2                                                NACA MR No. FJ4K30


     In order to determine the performance of the engine and the
characteristics of the induction system, bench tests were made at
simulated altitudes of 17,0 00 and 29,000 feet. Refrigerated air
was used to obtain altitude pressure and temperature at the top
deck of the carburetor, and the pressure at the engine discharge
was obtained by means of an evacuated exhaust line. The engine
speed was maintained at a constant value of 3000 rpm, and the tip
speed of the auxiliary-stage supercharger was varied from the min-
imum to the maximum value. In addition to measurements of engine
power and fuel and air consumptions, the pressure and the temper-
ature changes introduced by each component of the induction system
were determined.

     The performance of the superchargers was studied and compared
with the bench tests at sea level. The factors limiting engine
brake horsepower were noted and the engine-performance data were
extrapolated in order to estimate the power that could be obtained
if these limitations were removed. By means of an assumed relation
between engine power, air consumption, and manifold temperature, the
changes in the induction system required for the desired engine per-
formance were determined.

     The investigation reported herein was made at the NACA Cleve-
land laboratory from January to June 1944.


                             APPARATUS

     Test setup. - For the present tests, an Allison V-1710-93 engine
(fig. 1) was modified by replacing the extension shaft with an F-type
reduction gear. Figure 2, which is reproduced from an Allison man-
ual, shows how the auxiliary-stage supercharger impeller was driven
from the engine starts.r shaft through a universal joint, a hydraulic
coupling, and step-up gears. Independent control of the auxiliary-
stage impeller was obtained by regulating the amount of oil flowing
through the hydraulic coupling. A 2000-horsepower dynamometer absorbed
the power generated by the engine. Champion C3S spark plugs were
used for both tests. The fuel was AN-F-28, Amendment-i. -        -

     The engine induction system consists of a carburetor, an adapter
and inlet elbow, an auxiliary-stage supercharger, an interstage duct,
an engine-stage supercharger, and engine manifolds (fig. 3). A stan-
dard Bendix-Stromberg PT-13E9 carburetor was used. At the simulated
altitude of 29,000 feet, the mixture control was functioning improperly
and, in order to avoid the excessively lean mixture obtained, a new
automatic mixture control was installed for the tests at 17,000 feet.
At the same time, the diameter of the pistons was decreased 0.008 inch
MACA MR-No. E4K30                                                   3


and keystone piston rings were installed in order to avoid piston
seizure.

     The auxiliary-stage supercharger has a 15-bladed half-shrouded
aluminum impeller with an over-all diameter of 12 inches and a
separately machined steel inducer. The 11-vaned diffuser is cast
integrally with the inlet elbow and the front cover. The exit of
the scroll collector and the interstage duct connecting the super-
chargers have an elliptical cross section. The inlet of the engine-
stage supercharger is circular and is connected to the interstage
duct by flanges; this connection is fitted with a fuel baffle over-
hanging the supercharger-inlet section. A fuel nozzle is mounted
on top of the front cover of the engine-stage supercharger for the
injection of fuel. The 9-inch diameter engine-stage impeller is
similar to the auxiliary-stage impeller. The cight-vaned diffuser
of the engine-stage supercharger is cast integrally with the super-
charger front cover. A sharp 900 bend connects the scroll collector
with the center manifold of the engine, which in turn is divided into
four branch manifolds.

     Instrumentation. - The air flow throuh the induction system
was measured by a Bailey adjustable orifice in the refrigerated
inlet-air duct and the pressure drop across the orifice was deter-
mined by an MACA micromanonieter to 0.00 inch of water. Two iron-
constantan thermocouples immediately preceding the orifice mea'ured
the temperature of the air entering the orifice.

     With the exception of pressure surveys at the entrance of each
supercharger to check the flow distribution, all pressure and tem-
perature measurements (fig. 3) were taken in the same plane at each
station. Surveys should be made in order to obtain the most accu-
rate information on the effective total pressure at each station,
but making these surveys is hazardous.. Although the measurements
obtained do not give the exact state of the flow at these stations,
the variation of the performance characteristics 'and the flow condi-
tions for each component of the induction system with the auxiliary-
stage tip s peed and the altitude can be determined.

     Inasmuch as the engine-stage supercharger was driven by a fixed
gear, the impeller angular velocity and tip speed were calculated
directly from the gear ratio. Owing to the hydraulic coupling in
the drive of the auxiliary-stage supercharger, however, the speed of
this stage was measured by electronic equipment. A small rnagietic
pickup was mounted on the magnesium front cover of the auxiliary-
stage supercharger in the same radial plane as the steel inducer.
This device measures the number of vanes passing e given point per
second.

     Fuel flow into th engine was measured directly in pounds per
hour by a rotameter and engine speed by a calibrated tachometer.
A dynamometer connected to an NACA torque cell was used to deter-
mine engine torque and power. Knock conditions were indicated by
a cathode-ray oscillograph connected to Allison magnetostriction
pickups installed under each intake spark plug.

     All other engine operating conditions were measured on stand-
ard aircraft gages. Pressure readings in the induction system were
taken by mercury manometers and the corresponding temperatures were
read on a potentiometer.

     Performance characteristics. - The variation of load coeffi-
cient and volume flow with tip speed for the auxiliary-stage super-

greater than 800 feet per second the curve of volume flow approaches
a straight line, as would be expected from the corresponding con-
stant value of the load coefficient.

fact thaf the pressure measurements in the present tests were not

cient is approximately 8 percent higher and the adiabatic efficiency

the temperature rise in the present tests is higher. The effects of
heat transfer on increasing the temperature rise would probably be

                            Power Limits

     Incipient knocking of the engine was noted at 17,000 feet for a
manifold. temperature of nearly 2200 F and a manifold pressure of
approximately 55 inches of mercury absolute, which agree with those
8                                                  NACA PR No. E4K30


presented in reference 3. The knock-limited horsepower was 1228,
which was obtained when the slip of the auxiliary-stage hydraulic
coupling with a 6.85:1 gear ratio was approximately 15 percent. A
decrease in manifold temperature would not only increase the knock-
limited horsepower and permit the use of higher auxiliary-stage tip
speeds but also increase the horsepower output at the low tip speeds.
Increasing the speed of the auxiliary-stage impeller beyond the pre-
sent. maximum value, however, would also increase the engine air flow,
which already sli g htly exceeds the value at which the mixture begins
to lean out.

      The only limit of brake horsepower at 29,000 feet is the degree
of supercharging. The pressure and the temperature at the manifold
for the maximum power were 40.6 inches of mercury absolute and 2150 F,
respectively. Although the power can be increased by increasing the
tip speeds of the superchargers, little power can be gained unless
some method of charge-air cooling is provided to suppress detonation
(reference 3). For both altitudes, therefore, some form of charge-
air cooling is a necessary prerequisite for any increase in brake
horsepower. Reduction in the charge-air temperature can be accom-
plished by aftercooling, intercooling, coolant injection, or any com-
bination of the three.

      The curves of figure 13 may be used to investigate the modifi-
cations required to obtain the desired power at 17,000 and 29,000 feet.
The solid lines of this figure show the variation of weight flow of
air, manifold temperature and pressure, and brake horsepower with
tip speed over the range of test speeds. The dashed lines show the
variation of. these quantities at tip speeds higher than those tested;
the extrapolation is valid if the general trend at the low tip speeds
continues and the effects of detonation and flow capacity of the car-
buretor and the superchargers and the problems of fuel metering are
neglected.

60 percent charge-air cooling is available, the manifold temperature

coolant injection would be necessary. If the weight flow of air is

air per hour would flow. The critical mass-flow rate through the
PT-13E9 carburetor at 17,000 feet, determined from Bendix-Stromberg
tests, is approximately 12,800 pounds per hour. The mixture, however,
actually begins to lean out at approximately 10,500 pounds per hour.
The carburetor is therefore incapable of handling the desired flow.
Restriction of the flow can be avoided by using a larger carburetor,

       The maximum value of load coefficient at which the supercharger
can operate efficiently is 0.235 (reference 2). At 17,000 feet, the
maximum weight flow of air is therefore 14,-r30 pounds per hour, which
is 1450 pounds . of air per hour less than that required to develop
17 4 5 brake horsepower. If the carburetor is removed from in, front
of the auxiliary-stage supercharger, however, the capacity of the
auxiliary-stage supercharger will be more than ample for this power.
In order to approach a value of 1840 horsepower at 17,000 feet, there-
fore, it will be necessary to increase the auxiliary-stage gear ratio
to 8.08l, provide aftercooling, place the carburetor between the two
superchargers, and provide for coolant injection downstream from the
aftercooler.

a brake horsepower of approximately 1090, a manifold pressure of
approximately 46.3 inches of mercury absolute, and a manifold tem-

point would approximate 10,200 pounds per hour, whereas the critical

performance at 29,000 feet, the carburetor will have to be either

supercharger with th carbu'etor in its present position is approxi-

Thus, any modifications that will increase the brake horsepower to

imately 300 F may be obtained. The resulting increase in the density
of the charge air at each section of the induction system would increase
the weight flow for a given volume flow and thus extend the range of
the carburetor and the superchargers. The manifold pressure and

                          SUV!AR1 OF RESULTS

     Bench tests of the Allison V-1710-93 engine at simulated alti-
tudes of 17,000 and 29,000 feet gave the following results:

     1. The pressure ratio developed by each supercharger at a given
load coefficient and Mach number was similar to that obtained during
bench tests of these components at sea level.

     2. The temperature rise through the superchargers was consider-
ably different from that anticipated from the sea-level bench tests.
At high tip speeds of the auxiliary-stage supercharger, the over-all
temperature rise at 17,000 and 29,000 feet was 20 0 F and 5 0 F., res-
pectively, lower than the anticipated values.

     3. The manifold pressures, the manifold temperatures, and the
fuel-air ratios at which detonation occurred at 17,000 feet agreed
with those previously obtained with sea-level exhaust pressure.

     4. Charge-air cooling must be used if any appreciable increase
in power is to be obtained, particularly at 17,000 feet.

        . In order to obtain brake horsepowers approaching1840 at
17, 00 0 feet, the minimum gear ratios of the engine-stage and the
auxiliary-stage superchar gers should be 8.1:1 and 8.08:1, respectively.

     6. Either the capacity of the carburetor should be increased or,
preferably, the carburetor should be located at some other part of
the induction system.
